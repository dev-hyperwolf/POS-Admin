# Medicinal purchase limit — what the law says, and the one rule the software enforces

Date: 2026-09-17. Builds on `PURCHASE-LIMITS-PLAN-2026-09-17.md` §1.2, §1.3, §3.6 and §4.1 Q4; does
not repeat them. Everything here was re-read from the primary text today unless marked
**SECONDARY** (vendor pages, case summaries) or **NOT REACHED** (source offline).

Legal jargon, one line each, used below:
- **4 CCR** = the Department of Cannabis Control's regulations (Title 4, Cal. Code of Regulations).
- **H&S** = Health and Safety Code (the patient statutes from Prop 215 / SB 420). **B&P** = Business
  and Professions Code (the licensing statute, MAUCRSA). **R&T** = Revenue and Taxation Code.
- **MMIC** = the state Medical Marijuana Identification Card, issued by the county for the
  California Department of Public Health (CDPH). Voluntary. Nine-digit number, photo, expiry.
- **Physician's recommendation ("rec")** = a doctor's written recommendation to use cannabis.
  Enough to be a patient; **not** enough for the sales-tax exemption.
- **ISOR / FSOR** = the regulator's own written reasons when it adopted the rule (Initial / Final
  Statement of Reasons). They are the official explanation of what the words were meant to do.

---

## 0. The answer in one paragraph

The law gives a medicinal patient (or their caregiver buying for them) **one** daily purchase
limit: **8 ounces (226.8 g) of cannabis "in the form of dried mature flowers or the plant
conversion"**, plus 12 immature plants — and **no concentrate line at all**. The 8 g concentrate
figure exists only in the adult-use subsection. The regulator was asked twice in 2018 to spell out
how concentrates count for patients and answered, both times, that it would not: the amounts are
"case by case" and the retailer is "in the best position" to decide. Nothing since (2019 to the
July 2026 compilation, and the 2025–2026 rulemakings) has changed a word of it. So: **there is no
legal concentrate cap for patients; the retailer must set its own sensible measure inside the
8 oz.** A patient is anyone 18+ with a valid rec **or** an MMIC (either one), or the patient's
caregiver. **Only the MMIC** (plus a government ID) removes sales tax; a rec alone never does.

## 1. The regulation, verbatim — 4 CCR §15409 "Daily Limits"

Read today from two copies with identical wording: the DCC's official compilation "Medicinal &
Adult Use Cannabis Regulations, July 2026 — regulations in effect as of July 1, 2026", pp. 104–105
(<https://cdn.cannabis.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/08/dcc_regulations_20260701.pdf>) and
LII (<https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/4-CCR-15409>, quarterly mirror, read
2026-09-17).

> (a) A licensed retailer shall not sell more than the following amounts to a single adult-use
> cannabis customer in a single day: (1) 28.5 grams of non-concentrated cannabis. (2) 8 grams of
> cannabis concentrate as defined in Business and Professions Code section 26001, including cannabis
> concentrate contained in cannabis products. (3) 6 immature cannabis plants.
>
> (b) A licensed retailer shall not sell more than the following amounts to a single medicinal
> cannabis patient, or to a patient's primary caregiver purchasing medicinal cannabis on behalf of
> the patient, in a single day: (1) 8 ounces of medicinal cannabis in the form of dried mature
> flowers or the plant conversion as provided in Health and Safety Code section 11362.77. (2) 12
> immature cannabis plants.
>
> (c) Notwithstanding subsection (b) of this section, if a medicinal cannabis patient's valid
> physician's recommendation contains a different amount than the limits listed in this section,
> the medicinal cannabis patient may purchase an amount of medicinal cannabis consistent with the
> patient's needs as recommended by a physician and documented in the physician's recommendation.
>
> (d) The limits provided in subsection (a) and subsection (b) of this section shall not be
> combined to allow a customer to purchase cannabis goods in excess of any of the limits provided
> in this section.
>
> (e) For the purposes of this section, a licensed retailer shall be responsible for determining
> that the amount of cannabis concentrates found in manufactured cannabis products sold to
> customers comply with the requirements of this section.
>
> Authority cited: Section 26013, Business and Professions Code. Reference: Section 26012,
> Business and Professions Code; and Sections 11362.1 and 11362.77, Health and Safety Code.

History and currency:
- Adopted as 16 CCR §5409 by the Bureau of Cannabis Control (BCC); OAL approved and it took
  effect **2019-01-16** (Register 2019, No. 3). Renumbered to 4 CCR §15409 on **2021-07-14**
  "without regulatory effect" (Register 2021, No. 29) when the DCC consolidated the three agencies'
  rules. LII lists no later history; the DCC July 2026 compilation carries the same text.
- 2024–2026 rulemakings checked on <https://www.cannabis.ca.gov/cannabis-laws/rulemaking/>
  (read 2026-09-17): DCC-2025-01-R cultivation/sanitation (eff. 2026-07-01), DCC-2025-02-R animal
  products (eff. 2026-01-01), DCC-2025-03-R pesticide testing (eff. 2026-10-01), DCC-2026-01-R
  multipacks (comment closed 2026-04-13), DCC-2026-02-R track-and-trace updates (adds §15049.4 and
  §15404.1; comment closed 2026-07-20), DCC-2026-03-E separate A and M licences (emergency, eff.
  2026-06-04, expires 2026-12-02), DCC-2026-03-R group tagging. **None touches §15409.** Watch
  DCC-2026-02-R only because its new §15404.1 (COAs to customers) sits next to the retail-customer
  rules; it does not change limits.
- A word-search of the whole 235-page July 2026 compilation for any line containing "concentrat"
  together with "patient" or "medicinal" returns **zero** lines. The regulation is silent, not
  subtle.

What each subsection means for us, in plain words:
- (a) Adult-use: three separate buckets (28.5 g flower / 8 g concentrate / 6 plants).
- (b) Medicinal: one weight (8 oz = 226.8 g) plus plants. It names "dried mature flowers or the
  plant conversion" as the measure and points to H&S §11362.77 for that phrase.
- (c) A rec that states a larger amount overrides (b). The FSOR confirms the reading: "a patient
  may be sold an amount of cannabis goods consistent with the recommendation" (Appendix A p. 257).
- (d) A patient gets the medicinal limits **instead of** the adult-use ones, never both. The ISOR
  says this subsection exists precisely to answer "whether a medicinal patient may purchase the
  maximum amount allotted for medicinal cannabis goods in addition to the maximum amount allotted
  for adult-use cannabis goods … a medicinal patient may not" (ISOR p. 103).
- (e) Working out how much concentrate is in a gummy, a cart or an infused pre-roll is **our**
  job, for every customer type.

## 2. The statute behind (b) — H&S §11362.77 and People v. Kelly

H&S §11362.77 (leginfo, read 2026-09-17; last amended SB 94, Stats. 2017 ch. 27, eff. 2017-06-27;
<https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=HSC&sectionNum=11362.77>):
- (a) a patient or caregiver "may possess no more than eight ounces of dried cannabis per
  qualified patient", plus six mature or 12 immature plants per patient;
- (b) if a physician recommends that this "does not meet the qualified patient's medical needs",
  the patient may possess an amount "consistent with the patient's needs";
- (c) counties and cities may allow more;
- (d) "Only the dried mature processed flowers of female cannabis plant or the plant conversion
  shall be considered when determining allowable quantities of cannabis under this section."
- (e) card-holders, qualified patients and their caregivers may possess amounts consistent with
  the article.

**"Plant conversion" is not defined anywhere.** Not in H&S §11362.7 (definitions), not in 4 CCR
§15000, not in the 2018 ISOR, not in the 2019 FSOR. The 2008 Attorney General guidelines that
might gloss it were **NOT REACHED** (oag.ca.gov returned an HTML shell, not the PDF). Read in
context — a possession statute that also counts plants — it most naturally means "the weight of
cannabis a patient's plants convert into", i.e. it lets a grower's harvest be measured against the
8 oz. It does **not** on its face say anything about extracts. Two readings are possible for a
retailer and neither has an official answer:
1. Literal: the 8 oz is measured in dried flower; concentrate is simply not what (b) measures, so
   the only thing that caps a patient's concentrate is the rec (c) and our own judgment (e).
2. Inclusive: concentrate is "converted" into the 8 oz and eats the same 226.8 g pool, gram for
   gram or by some equivalence.
Nobody has ever published a California flower-to-concentrate equivalence. Colorado and other
states do; California does not.

**People v. Kelly (2010) 47 Cal.4th 1008, S164830, decided 2010-01-21** (opinion read from a
Westlaw reprint hosted at lerachapter.org — the reprint is **SECONDARY** hosting of a primary
text). The Supreme Court held the §11362.77 amounts cannot be used to cut down a patient's defence
under the Compassionate Use Act (a voter initiative the Legislature could not narrow), but left the
section standing as a "safe harbor": possess within the amounts and you are protected. **For the
software this changes nothing.** Kelly is about criminal liability of a *patient*; the BCC was
asked about Kelly during rulemaking and answered that "the regulation that sets a limit on the
amount of cannabis goods that a retailer may sell to a single patient [is] very different from a
law that imposes criminal liability on medicinal patients … The Bureau is authorized to set a sales
limit on retailers to protect public safety" (FSOR Appendix A p. 257, comment 3407; Kelly noted at
Appendix A p. 493). The retailer's cap is the regulation, full stop.

## 3. What the regulator said when it wrote the rule (primary; the part that settles the question)

Sources: BCC **Initial Statement of Reasons**, July 2018, 567 pp. (archived copy of
`bcc.ca.gov/law_regs/cannabis_isr.pdf`, capture 2018-12-21) and BCC **Final Statement of
Reasons and Updated Informative Digest with Appendices A–D**, dated 2019-01-15, 1,081 pp.
(archived copy of `bcc.ca.gov/law_regs/cannabis_fsor.pdf`, capture 2019-02-23). bcc.ca.gov itself
is offline (all URLs return no response, 2026-09-17); the archive captures are the only copies.

- **Why 8 oz** (ISOR pp. 102–103): subsection (b) "mirror[s] … the legal possession limits for
  medicinal cannabis patients found in Health and Safety Code section 11362.77"; the purpose of
  the section is "to prevent retailers from selling an amount … which would result in the customer
  being in violation of the legal possession limits". The ISOR's description of (b) never mentions
  concentrate, edibles or manufactured products. (The ISOR describes only (a)–(d); (e) did not
  exist yet.)
- **Asked to define concentrate limits, the Bureau refused** (FSOR Appendix A p. 256, comments
  189.10, 922.5, 1711.1–1711.6, 3458.1: "Commenters request that the daily limits for concentrates
  be specified further. Further guidance on how to apply the limits to cannabis in concentrated
  form found in products is necessary."): *"The Bureau disagrees with this comment. The daily sales
  limits are based on the legal possession limits found in the Health and Safety Code. … However,
  the amounts will need to be determined on a case by case basis. The retailer is in the best
  position to make that determination prior to the sale."* Same answer to comment 297.3 ("not
  possible to determine the amount of cannabis concentrate used in a manufactured cannabis
  product").
- **Why (e) was added** (FSOR p. 38, 15-day changes): "This section adds subsection (e), which
  clarifies that the retailer is responsible for determining the amount of cannabis concentrates
  sold to customers in any form. In some instances, the amount of cannabis concentrates used in a
  manufactured product is not listed on the packaging. This has led to confusion …" Retailers who
  objected were told "Retailers are in a good position to know or learn the amount of cannabis
  concentrates used in the cannabis goods they sell" (Appendix C pp. 327–328).
- **Weight, not THC**: a request to set limits by "the amount of active ingredients" rather than
  weight was rejected (Appendix A p. 257, comment 1625.39; Appendix D p. 26, comment 4115.37).
- **Caregivers for several patients**: "a primary caregiver may purchase cannabis goods on behalf
  of patients they are providing care for" — the limit is per patient (Appendix A p. 255, comment
  1640.17).
- **Patients above 8 oz** (Appendix A p. 257, comment 1148.3): the Bureau "agrees … a patient may
  be sold an amount of cannabis goods consistent with the recommendation."

**What they do not say, anywhere:** that a patient's concentrate is capped at 8 g; that a patient's
concentrate is uncapped; what "plant conversion" means; any gram or THC-mg equivalence. The
regulator deliberately left the measure to the retailer.

DCC's consumer page (<https://cannabis.ca.gov/consumers/whats-legal/>, undated, read 2026-09-17)
says only that medicinal users "Have higher possession limits" and can "Buy more cannabis each day
if their physician recommends it". No DCC FAQ gives a number. The former
`cannabis.ca.gov/consumers/medicinal-cannabis/` page is a 404.

Plain-language consequence: **a valid rec and an MMIC are equally good for "is this person a
patient"**. The rec is the underlying document in both cases (an MMIC is only issued on a rec).
The retailer's duty at sale is "confirm age, identity, and physician's recommendation" — the
statute does not say "phone the Medical Board" for ordinary sales. The §15411 phone-the-board
procedure is written for free goods; it is also the only procedure the DCC has ever written down,
which is why the plan (§3.6) adopts it as the verification standard for every medical designation.
That is a policy choice, and a conservative one, not a legal requirement — it stays.

Ages: adult-use 21+ (B&P §26140(a)(1), 4 CCR §15404(a)); medicinal 18+ (§26140(c)(3), §15404(b)).
A patient under 18 cannot buy at retail in person; their caregiver (a parent) buys for them.

## 5. Tax — the one place the MMIC and the rec are different

R&T §34011, operative text (leginfo chapter view, read 2026-09-17,
<https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=RTC&division=2.&title=&part=14.5.&chapter=2.&article=>):

> (f) The sales and use taxes imposed by Part 1 (commencing with Section 6001) shall not apply to
> retail sales of medicinal cannabis, medicinal cannabis concentrate, edible medicinal cannabis
> products, or topical cannabis as those terms are defined in Division 10 … of the Business and
> Professions Code when a qualified patient or primary caregiver for a qualified patient provides
> their card issued under Section 11362.71 of the Health and Safety Code and a valid
> government-issued identification card.

**Correction to the plan:** the plan cites this as §34011(g). It was (g) in the Prop 64 original;
leginfo today shows two versions of §34011 — the AB 195 (2022) version, which became inoperative
2023-04-01 and does not carry the sentence, and the AB 141 (2021) version that "would resume
operation, by reversion" on that date and carries it as **(f)**. The excise tax itself now lives
in §34011.2 (AB 195; rate changes AB 564, Stats. 2025 ch. 127). Cite **R&T §34011(f)**.

CDTFA, "Tax Guide for Cannabis Businesses — Retailers" (<https://www.cdtfa.ca.gov/industry/cannabis/retailers.htm>,
undated, read 2026-09-17), the agency that audits the exemption:
- "To obtain the exemption, qualified patients or their primary caregiver must furnish their valid
  Medical Marijuana Identification Card (MMIC) issued by the California Department of Public Health
  (CDPH) and a valid government-issued identification card (ID) at the time of purchase."
- Records per exempt sale: "The purchaser's nine-digit ID number and expiration date, as shown on
  the qualified patient's or primary caregiver's unexpired Medical Marijuana Identification Card"
  and "The related sales invoice or other original record of sale". "Retailers may verify the
  validity of the nine-digit ID number on the CDPH website."
- "This exemption only applies to sales and use tax and does not apply to the cannabis excise tax."

So: **rec only → medicinal limits, full sales tax, full excise. MMIC + government ID → medicinal
limits, no sales/use tax, excise still charged.** R&T §6414 (use-tax exemption for *donated*
medicinal cannabis) is a different rule and its patient definition (§6414(e)) covers rec-holders —
it does not widen the retail-sale exemption. Do not let anyone argue from §6414 to §34011.

## 7. The recommended enforcement rule (plain language)

**Adult-use customer (21+, government ID).** Three buckets, none combinable: 28.5 g of
non-concentrated cannabis; 8 g of concentrate, counting the concentrate inside edibles, carts,
tinctures and infused pre-rolls; 6 immature plants. Unchanged from the plan.

**Medicinal customer (18+, government ID, and a valid rec or a valid MMIC; or the caregiver of
such a patient).** The medicinal limits replace the adult-use ones (§15409(d)); a patient never
gets 28.5 g + 226.8 g.
1. **One pool of 226.8 g (8 oz) per patient per day**, into which every cannabis product is
   counted by the same gram measure the adult-use engine already uses: flower by net cannabis
   weight; concentrate and manufactured products by extract weight or THC mg ÷ 1000, whichever is
   larger (plan Q3 option 1). This is the Cova reading. It is the only reading under which we can
   **never** be shown to have sold more than "8 ounces" whatever "plant conversion" turns out to
   mean, and selling less than the law allows is always lawful.
2. **Plants: 12 immature.**
3. **Concentrate: the law sets no cap for patients.** Say so on the screen and in the audit log.
   The software nevertheless applies a **house cap on concentrate for patients, default 8 g/day,
   held in a setting (`medicinal_concentrate_house_cap_g`), not in code**, for three reasons: the
   regulator told retailers the concentrate amount is theirs to decide "case by case"; no
   conversion factor exists, so a patient walking out with 200 g of extract would be defended only
   by our own reading of an undefined phrase; and 8 g is a number an inspector already recognises.
   The cap is labelled "store policy" in the customer-facing message, never "state law". The owner
   can raise it later without a release (see the question in §10).
4. **Physician-stated amount (§15409(c)) overrides both the pool and the house cap.** If the rec
   states a daily or per-purchase amount, that documented amount becomes the patient's pool (and,
   if it names concentrate, the concentrate cap) for as long as the rec is valid. Only a verifier
   with the `compliance` permission can enter it, and only with the document reference (plan
   §3.6). No rec amount, no override; no verbal claims.
5. **Caregiver:** limits are per named patient; a caregiver buying for two patients has two pools
   and must tell us which patient each item is for. No DCC caregiver-verification rule exists; we
   record the patient link and the caregiver's own ID (§15411(b)(1)(A)(ii) standard).
6. **Expiry:** a rec or MMIC past its date drops the person to adult-use (and blocks an 18–20
   year-old entirely). A rec has no statutory term; use the expiry the physician wrote, else one
   year from issue, matching the MMIC's statutory one-year term and the §15411 annual re-check.

## 8. Decision table — customer type × bucket → daily limit

| Bucket | Adult-use | Medicinal (rec or MMIC), no amount on the rec | Medicinal, rec states an amount |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-concentrated cannabis (flower, non-infused pre-rolls) | 28.5 g | counts into the 226.8 g pool | counts into the rec's stated pool |
| Concentrate, incl. concentrate inside products (carts, extracts, hash/kief, edibles, tinctures, topicals, infused pre-roll's infused part) | 8 g | counts into the 226.8 g pool **and** ≤ house cap (default 8 g; setting) | counts into the stated pool; house cap replaced by the rec's concentrate amount if it names one, else unchanged |
| Immature plants | 6 | 12 | 12 (or as the rec states) |
| Medical-only products (§17304: >100 mg edibles, >1,000 mg concentrates/topicals) | **refused** | allowed, counted as above | allowed, counted as above |
| Combining columns | never (§15409(d)) | never | never |

Both limits are per legal day (plan Q1) and per person across all our licences and channels
(plan Q2). A same-day void returns allowance; a return does not (plan Q6).

## 9. Data the software stores (minimised)

Per medicinal status record (extends plan §3.6): `kind` (rec | mmic_patient | mmic_caregiver |
caregiver_no_card); `patient_identity_id` (for caregivers, the patient's record); `expires_on`;
`stated_daily_g` and `stated_concentrate_g` (nullable, verifier-only); for an MMIC, the nine-digit
number and expiry **in full** (CDTFA needs them on the record — encrypted at rest, last four shown);

`next_reverify_on`; `document_ref` to the IDV store. Store **nothing** about the medical condition,
no SSN, no image inline. Keep with the seven-year set (§15037). Each exempt sale line carries
`tax_exempt_reason = mmic` plus the MMIC record id, so the CDTFA record is a join, not a copy.

## 11. Owner question (one; it supersedes plan Q4)

**Q. What concentrate cap should the software apply to medicinal patients, given that the law sets
none?** Pick one; the first is recommended.
1. **8 g per day as store policy, lifted to whatever a physician's recommendation documents.**
   Pros: never over-sells under any reading; an inspector recognises the number; the higher amount
   still exists for patients whose doctor wrote it; one setting, changeable later. Cons: some
   patients buying ten carts will be told no and asked for a rec that states an amount.
2. No concentrate cap; only the 226.8 g pool. Pros: the literal regulation and what Treez does.
   Cons: a patient could take 226 g of extract in a day on our reading of an undefined phrase;
   we would be the test case.
3. A derived flower-equivalent cap: 226.8 g ÷ 3.56 (the state's own adult-use ratio of 28.5 g
   flower to 8 g concentrate) ≈ 63 g concentrate per day. Pros: a defensible arithmetic; far above
   any normal patient need. Cons: invented; harder to explain to staff and inspectors than 8 g.
4. Treat every patient as adult-use for concentrate (8 g, no rec override). Pros: simplest.
   Cons: ignores §15409(c), which the regulator confirmed in writing; refuses lawful sales.

No second question is needed. Everything else above is a mechanic and is decided here.
