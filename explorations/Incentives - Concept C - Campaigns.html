Bounty — Concept C: Campaigns

DESIGN MOCKUP — every number is illustrative, nothing is read from the API · Hyperwolf Bounty — name picked by the owner 2026-09-07; one incentive is a bounty
Concept C · the organizing idea

Bounties are the product. Standings are a property of a bounty — not the front door.

Every other way of arranging this module makes the leaderboard the home screen and treats bounties as configuration hanging off it. This one inverts that: the landing screen is Active now, a card per running campaign, and a rank only ever appears inside the campaign that produced it. A budtender does not want to know they are second in the abstract — they want to know they are second in the STIIIZY pod push, that first place is eleven units ahead, that it ends Sunday at close, and that the brand is paying for it. That is one card, and there are four of them.

It borrows its shape wholesale from engage/screen-campaigns.jsx — a module sidebar with grouped nav, a StatTile KPI row, and a responsive grid of campaign cards each carrying its own status pill, channel chip and per-card metric strip. The bounty detail borrows engage/screen-loyalty.jsx’s program-detail spine (crumb, big title, mono id line, KPI strip, then stacked cards). The Data screen is engage/screen-ops.jsx’s Integrations table, one row per store × source. The builder is the Engage flow-builder pattern: a stepper, one decision per step, and a summary sentence that rewrites itself as you go.

Who it serves best. The manager running promotions and the brand rep funding them. The status board — draft → pending approval → active → ended → settled — and the approval inbox are first-class here rather than buried in a settings screen, because with brand-funded campaigns the approval is the operational moment. The budtender is served second but not badly: they open the app to four cards, each of which tells them their own position without a second click.

What it trades away. The all-store leaderboard is one click deeper than any other concept would put it — you reach it from Standings, and Standings is the fifth item in the sidebar. If your primary user is a budtender comparing themselves against the whole chain every hour, this is the wrong shape. It also spends its landing screen’s vertical budget on the Snaps highlight row, which pushes the fourth and fifth bounty cards below the fold on a 900px laptop.

Why Snaps sits on the landing screen anyway. §3.7 lets a snap link to a bounty. That link is the whole argument for putting education in this module at all rather than in Engage: learn it, then sell it. A story circle whose ring is accent-coloured is unread and its caption names the bounty it feeds. Reading it takes ninety seconds and it is the only thing on the screen that changes what a budtender knows rather than what they know about themselves.

Honesty, in this shape. There is no fixture data in this module, so the labelling is about connection and freshness, exactly as §5.3 requires. Every bounty card footer carries the freshness of the source scoring it; a card whose store has no configured source wears a no data source badge instead of a rank, because a rank computed from nothing is worse than no rank. Unattributed sales get a row in every standings view, never a silent exclusion, and a projected earning never wears the same treatment as a settled one.

Composites used here, and what they are made of: the bounty card = Card + Pill + BarMeter + Avatar; the Snaps circle = Avatar in a 2px ring + a caption; the status board column = Card elevation="sunken" holding Card chips; the summary sentence = plain text with accentSoft/accentText spans. Nothing here needs a new atom.

Where it differs from the siblings

A leaderboard-first concept answers “where do I stand?”. This one answers “what am I playing today, and how is it going?”. The practical consequence is that a bounty with no participants and no sales still has a card — it is a real object with a status — whereas in a standings-first shape it is invisible until someone scores. That matters most in the first hour of a new spiff, which is exactly when a manager is checking whether it is working.

The landing screen · route #/

One card per running campaign. Your position lives inside the card that produced it.

The grid is engage/screen-campaigns.jsx’s repeat(auto-fit, minmax(320px, 1fr)) card grid with a different payload: instead of sent/delivery/CTR/revenue, each card carries a top three, your own row with a BarMeter, the funding source, and the time left. The Snaps row across the top is the “learn it, then sell it” pairing — an accent ring means unread, and the caption names the bounty the story feeds.

Route #/contests · the status board and the approval inbox

A campaign is an object with a lifecycle, so the list is a board, not a table.

Draft → pending approval → active → ended → settled, left to right, with cancelled behind a filter. active and ended are derived from the clock and never stored, so a campaign moves between those two columns without anyone writing anything — the board says so under the column heading rather than pretending they are equal transitions. Brand-funded campaigns must pass through pending approval; that column is the manager’s inbox and it carries the only irreversible-feeling action in the module.

Route #/contests/new · the guided flow

Six steps, one decision each, and a sentence that rewrites itself as you answer.

The shape is Engage’s flow builder: a stepper across the top, one panel at a time, and a persistent right rail. What the right rail holds is the argument for this concept — a plain-English summary sentence assembled from the answers so far, with each answered fragment highlighted. A manager who cannot read the sentence has not built the campaign they think they built, and that is a cheaper failure to catch here than in the audit trail after settlement. The five kinds in §3.4 (spiff, bounty, team_goal, store_vs_store, aov_goal) are the first step, and choosing one changes which later steps appear at all.

Route #/standings · the deliberate trade-off

One screen: the leaderboard and the store race, with the all-store view one click away.

This is where the concept pays for its landing screen. Standings is the third sidebar item rather than the front door, and the All stores scope is a segment inside it — two clicks from opening the app rather than zero. In exchange, a budtender who only wants their own position never has to come here at all: it is already on every campaign card. What lives here is the question a card cannot answer — across everything, not one campaign, where does this floor stand — with period and metric controls, and the store race underneath.

Route #/earnings · budtender and manager

A wallet-shaped list: balance at the top, then one row per thing you earned and how firm it is.

Two states matter and the design has to keep them visibly apart: projected (the campaign has not settled, the number can still move) and settled (a real row in the points ledger, written once per bounty · round · person). A third marker, recorded paid, is bookkeeping only — a manager noting that they paid it by their own means, with who and when. No money moves in this module and no screen here implies otherwise.

This marker is bookkeeping and nothing else
Recording a payment writes one ledger row saying who marked it, when, and why. It moves no money, contacts no payroll system, and can be reversed with an adjustment row. If the owner would rather not have even the marker, it is one kind to drop from the schema.
The wallet rows are Card padding={0} with hairline-separated rows — the “Needs attention” pattern from pos/screen-home.jsx, re-pointed at money. Projected rows carry --info, settled rows --good, adjustments --warnText; the amount column is the only place the colour is spent.
Routes #/learn and #/learn/new · Snaps

A story player, because a snap is read standing up between customers.

Cards advance on a tap, a row of progress dots says how many are left, and the whole thing fits a phone-width column so the same component works on the register screen and on a manager’s desktop. A plain snap scores nothing — it is content. A snap with a quiz pays out on a pass, which is the SparkPlug “Courses” mechanic, and the payout is points rather than dollars so it never mixes with campaign money in the ledger. The link to a bounty is what makes the pairing on the landing screen work: learn it, then sell it.

Route #/goals · the absorbed AOV goal

The AOV goal becomes one campaign kind, and keeps its own screen because it is the one that never ends.

Every other campaign has a window and a settlement. An AOV goal is a standing number: a store default that everyone inherits, per-person overrides where a manager has set one, and an append-only audit of every change. That is the existing pos/screen-aov.jsx manager card, moved out of the POS Home slot and given room. The API contract underneath is unchanged — /api/aov/* is served from the new ledger with byte-compatible shapes — so nothing breaks while the rest of this module is built.

The rule these four share

Each state answers the same three questions in the same order: what is on screen, why it is that and not the real thing, and what you can do next. A state that answers only the first is the failure this estate keeps re-fixing — the fixture leaderboard that looked live, the green tick printed for every member, the half-empty table that only got noticed because the job also happened to time out.
