Concept D · organizing idea

Verification happens at a counter and in a checkout, not in an analyst's queue. So the two surfaces that face a customer come first, and the console that faces an analyst is the back office behind them.

Three of the four concepts start from the verifications table, because that is what a vendor console looks like. Concept D starts from the floor. The home screen is a now board: five columns — West Hollywood, Lake Elsinore, Corona, Long Beach and the website — each showing the sessions in flight as large status tiles you can read from across a back room, plus one lane at the bottom for the only thing that needs a person. Under it sits engine health and today's approvals and declines. A manager opening Verify sees the store, not the database.

The counter capture flow is designed for a tablet stood on the counter with the associate on one side and the customer on the other, both able to read it. Guides are big, the status word is 27px, and there are exactly three endings: Approved — welcome back, Needs a look — an analyst is on it, and Can't verify today. No score is shown to the customer, no reason code, no vocabulary they would have to ask about. The associate gets one extra line the customer does not need to read.

The website funnel is the second customer surface: sign-up, document, selfie, decision — with the drop-off between each step, because the site is where most sessions actually live today. The hosted capture page is the same flow in a phone frame, iframe-embeddable from www.hyperwolf.com with no postMessage, plus a Didit-compatible status facade so the site's existing 8-second poll keeps working on day one.

Everything else — verifications, session detail, review, people, workflows, lists, integrate, usage, settings — is a second tier on the rail, laid out plainly as forms and tables with no invention. That is the trade. What it wins: daily operations. A manager and an associate can use this on shift without training, and "who is waiting" is never more than a glance away. What it costs: analyst density. An analyst working a queue of forty gets a lane list, not a dense grid; the review screen is deliberately one-session-at-a-time with keyboard shortcuts, and reviewers who want twelve rows on screen at once will find Concepts A–C better.

Built out of the existing atoms, named where they appear: a status tile is Card + Pill; the human lane is DataTable with a Sheet on row click; engine health is StatTile + Spark; the capture screens are Card + PBtn size="xl" + canvas guides; the funnel is a stack of BarMeter; workflows are Field/Switch/Seg/Stepper in a plain form; every table is DataTable or HDTable. No new primitive is invented anywhere in this concept.

The three endings, in the customer's words
Approved
Screen says “You're good — welcome back.” Status literal stored: Approved. Check-in continues.
Needs a look
Screen says “Needs a look — an analyst is on it.” Status literal stored: In Review, with the reason codes. The customer is told roughly how long, not why.
Can't verify today
Screen says “We can't verify this today.” Status literal stored: Declined. No detail on screen — the reason is in the session, for the analyst and the audit trail.
The ten stored literals never change and are never softened in the database: Not Started · In Progress · Awaiting User · In Review · Approved · Declined · Resubmitted · Abandoned · Expired · Kyc Expired. Only the screen copy is plain.

What the board does not do
It does not rank stores against each other and it does not show a per-associate number. Verification throughput is not a performance metric and turning it into one would push associates to wave people through. The only comparison on the board is a store against its own quiet register.
Screen 2 · the tablet on the counter

One tablet, two readers. The associate is holding the conversation; the customer is watching the same screen — so nothing on it can need explaining.

Every frame below is the same tablet at 1024×768, drawn at a legible size. The customer-facing half is the big word and the guide. The associate's half is the thin bar at the bottom: the session number, the store, and one line of what to do next. Scores, reason codes and node names never appear here — they live in the session detail, tab 6.

The flow is start → front → back (PDF417) → passive selfie → active challenge → processing → outcome, with a retry branch, a returning-customer shortcut, and an engine-down state. Nothing passes silently and nothing is dropped: every attempt writes an event with its score, and the capture client never sets a status — only the engine's signed callback and an analyst do.

Mounted through pos/checkin-verify-seam.jsx — a new file that hangs IdvCapture off the check-in modal's existing document step via window.HW_CHECKIN. The Register screen is never modified. All imagery on this page is drawn in CSS; there are no external images anywhere in this file.

Engine down — the honest state

Zero third parties means there is no vendor to fall back to. When the engine is unreachable the screen says so plainly, and the manual path is drawn but off, because whether an associate may eyeball a physical ID is a ruling only the owner can make.

Why the button is grey and not missing

Hiding the manual path would make the decision invisible. Drawing it disabled, with the label “Off — needs an owner ruling”, puts the open question in front of the person who can close it. It is escalation §11.3 and it has three possible answers:

  • Fail closed — no check-in while the engine is down. This is the build default and what the frame shows.
  • Manual path — the associate confirms they looked at the physical ID; recorded as a manual decision, assurance T1-manual, re-verified on the next visit.
  • Manager-only manual path — the same, but the button needs a manager PIN.

Whichever is chosen, it is a workflow flag, not a code change, and it is audited either way. Until then the button stays disabled and honest.

Screen 3 · the other customer surface

The website is where nearly every session actually happens. So it gets its own screen, shaped like a funnel, not like a table.

www.hyperwolf.com creates a session at sign-up and again at checkout, drops the customer into an iframe, and polls for a status every eight seconds. This screen shows what that funnel does: how many started, how many made it past each step, and where the ones who left, left. It is the screen you open when the question is “are people getting stuck”, which is a different question from “what happened to this person”.

The total is real: 154 ID Verification checks this month, inside Didit's 500 free tier. The step split is drawn — Didit exposes no per-step drop-off, so today's funnel cannot be reconstructed. Once Verify owns the capture page, every step is an event and this screen is measured rather than modelled.

The Didit-compatible facade

The site polls GET didit/retrieve/status every eight seconds and reads a status literal. Verify keeps that endpoint shape and returns the same ten literals, so the site keeps working on the day it is pointed at us — three env vars and one CSP host. No site release is needed to switch.

→ 200

Same status literals, same decision arrays keyed by node_id, same X-Signature-V2 HMAC over a canonicalised body with a 300 s window and event_id idempotency. An exported Verify session is byte-comparable with a Didit one, so the console, the exports and the site all need zero translation.
One open decision on this screenneeds a ruling
“In Review” completes checkout today
The site treats In Review as good enough to finish an order. That is a policy hole, not a nuance — a session an analyst later declines has already sold product.

The facade returns the literal either way. What changes is what the site does with it:

Keep it
Fast checkout, review afterwards. Someone has to own the decline-after-sale case.
Fix it
The site waits for Approved. Slower checkout, no sold-then-declined orders.
Escalation §11.2. Until it is ruled, the facade's default is to translate In Review to “keep waiting” — the safer of the two, and reversible with one setting.
Screen 4 · back office

Review is a lane, not a grid — one session at a time, with the keyboard doing the work.

This is where Concept D pays for its floor-first bias. An analyst working a long queue would rather see twelve rows at once; here they get a lane of five, each row carrying the reason in words next to the code, and the session opens in a drawer over the queue rather than replacing it. The bet is that at ~150 sessions a month the queue is almost always short, and legibility beats density. If the volume ever changes, this is the first screen that would need to.

Real: the queue is 0 right now — Didit shows nothing to review and nothing in the last 30 days. The lane below is drawn to show the shape; in the real build an empty queue renders EmptyState, never a zero.

Screen 5 · back office

The session. Everything the engine saw, everything it scored, and every claim it is not entitled to make.

This is the one screen where numbers are shown without softening — an analyst is being asked to overrule a model, and they cannot do that on a summary. Each decision node carries its score, the threshold it was measured against, the model that produced it, and a caption saying what that model is and is not. The liveness caption says open model · uncertified because no open passive-liveness model is ISO 30107-3 certified, and pretending otherwise on a screen an analyst trusts would be the worst thing this module could do.

Imported sessions look different on purpose. A Didit session shows “Verified by Didit, imported” and is excluded from every engine-accuracy panel — it did not come from our engine and cannot be evidence about it.

Age rule

Recreational. 21 and over, read from the barcode date of birth. Under → Declined with UNDER_AGE.
MED_18_CARD
Medical. 18 and over plus a valid medical card. The engine can verify the age; it cannot verify the card.
MED_18_CARD needs a ruling first
Which system says a customer is medical — a Blaze member flag passed in expected_details, or a photo of the card reviewed by an analyst? Until that is answered, selecting it would create a rule with nothing behind it. Escalation §11.4.
Liveness method
PASSIVE
Two seconds of frames, nothing asked of the customer. Fastest. Weakest against a replayed video.
ACTIVE_3D
Passive frames, then a server-nonce head turn. The nonce is what makes a pre-recorded clip fail. Current.
FLASHING
Screen-flash reflection sequence. Strongest signal against a printed or screen-held face; hardest on customers in bright rooms.
No open passive-liveness model is ISO 30107-3 certified, and this build does not claim one is. The compensation is the nonce, the flash reflection, capture-path integrity signals, 1:N face search and a human — and the score carries the caption open model · uncertified everywhere it is shown.

What each screen does when there is nothing, when it is still coming, and when it did not come at all.

Verify has a fourth case the other apps do not: the backend can be up while the engine is down. Those are different outages with different consequences — one means the console is broken, the other means the register cannot verify anyone — so they are two badges, never one.
